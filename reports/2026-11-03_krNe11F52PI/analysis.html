1. Thesis and stance

Ben says plainly this is not a political video: he is not pushing any view and wants only to see what markets did under different outcomes so viewers can set expectations. The tone is neutral and data-heavy, with repeated caveats that the data set is small (few observations, large standard deviations) and that average does not mean must-happen.

3. Levels

No price levels. Statistics he quotes (figures may be garbled by translation):

  • Annual S&P return by regime since 1953: Democratic president + Republican Congress median close to 20%; Republican sweep average 13.3%, median 13.6%; Republican president + Democratic Congress average 4.9%, median 4%.
  • Including data back to 1928: Republican sweep average 9.8% and Democratic sweep 9% (medians 12.4 and 11.3, assignment unclear in the captions); Republican president with divided Congress and Republican president with Democratic Congress give lower returns, partly because the Great Depression is included.
  • Worst years under Democratic sweeps cited: 1931 about -47%, 1937 about -39%, 2008 about -38.5%, 1974 about -29.7%, 1930 about -28%, 2002 about -23%. (He mentions 1937 under a Democratic president and Congress; the other years' regime labels are not clearly stated.)
  • Sharpe ratio by term: highest Lyndon Johnson 1.96, then Roosevelt (1945 start), then Clinton third; worst Nixon's second term, Sharpe -1.6 and Sortino -2.15.
  • S&P since inauguration: now about +26% vs about +20% at the same point of Trump's first term.

5. Reasoning chains

  • Regime matters because prediction markets favour a divided or Democratic Congress, which flips the current Republican sweep (historically good) toward combinations with lower average returns.
  • But he stresses it is not that bad: average returns in the worst combo are still positive, and bad years cluster in a handful of episodes (1931, 1937, 1974, 2002, 2008), not one outlier.
  • Dispersion: Republican regimes show more extreme Sharpe outcomes than Democratic; the three highest-volatility points were Republican president + Democratic Congress, 1987 and 2020 (the pandemic).
  • Term comparison: Trump 2 is ahead of Biden at this stage because the market was in a bear market then; Trump 1 beat Obama 2 but Obama 1 beat Trump 1.
  • Inauguration-aligned: with a Republican president and Congress the market tends to dip before the midterms, bounce a bit, drop more, then continue; with a Republican president and divided Congress it drops more and is less bullish 6–9 months later.
  • For BTC he says the weaker Trump 2 / Biden returns likely reflect the post-2020 macro problems (inflation, labour market) that did not weigh on earlier cycles.

6. Macro and cross-market context

Equities: S&P near the same relative level as in Trump's first term, with a Q1 2026 correction timed like the Q1 2018 correction; the main difference is the early-2025 tariff drop. Crypto: Ben notes the market is obsessed with every $5,000 BTC move while BTC returns in this presidency are low versus past ones. He mentions the Brazil election surprise as a reminder that polls and prediction markets can miss, and says markets dislike uncertainty and prefer a known result.

7. Historical comparisons

  • Labelled as comparisons, not forecasts: 2018 (same Republican-sweep-to-divided-Congress sequence; Q1 correction); 1937 and 1931 Democratic-regime bear markets (to show sweeps can also fail); Nixon second term (worst Sharpe).
  • Trump 1, Obama 1/2 and Biden term-paths versus Trump 2 for S&P and BTC.

8. If-then conditions

  • If Democrats take both chambers (Republican president), then historically average forward S&P returns are the lowest of the combinations (about +4% over 12 months), though still positive on average.
  • If the 2018 template holds, a deeper correction after a Q1 dip is possible (he says ‘perhaps’).
  • If BTC follows the Biden term, a recovery would start in 2027.

9. Doubts, hedges, caveats

  • Data are few and standard deviations large; he says averages should not be over-read.
  • He does not know the results and says they might take days to be known; he stresses he cannot trust polls (Brazil example).
  • The Z-score scorecard chart is hard to read and he may fix it.
